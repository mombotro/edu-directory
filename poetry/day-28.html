Day 28 - Sestina

Sestina:
The sestina is one of the hardest forms in poetry. It was invented by medieval troubadours and is built from six words that repeat at the ends of lines. A sestina has six stanzas of six lines each, followed by a three-line closing stanza called the envoi (say "EN-voy"). No lines rhyme. Instead, the six end-words come back in a shifting order.

Number your six end-words 1 to 6. Each stanza ends with them in this order:

Stanza 1: 1 2 3 4 5 6
Stanza 2: 6 1 5 2 4 3
Stanza 3: 3 6 4 1 2 5
Stanza 4: 5 3 2 6 1 4
Stanza 5: 4 5 1 3 6 2
Stanza 6: 2 4 6 5 3 1
Envoi: uses all six words, two to a line. Lines usually END in 5, 3, 1, and each also holds 2, 4, 6.

Directions: Choose six words that can be used many ways (nouns work best, such as river, door, bread, and words with more than one meaning are great). Write a sestina of 39 lines. This is a big project, so work on a few lines at a time. It is fine if the sestina does not make perfect sense in every stanza. The challenge is to let the repeated words take you somewhere you did not plan to go.
Example (written for this backup, not a student example). End-words: 1 river, 2 stone, 3 light, 4 bread, 5 window, 6 home.

We walked down to the river
at dusk, each carrying one smooth stone;
the sky held on to its last light.
My mother had wrapped some bread
and left a lamp in the window
so we would know the way home.

The long way back is still the way home.
A heron stood and studied the river,
then turned its head toward a lit window.
I counted the ripples left by a stone,
then tore a corner off the bread
and fed it to the last of the light.

Some evenings all the town turns into light,
porch by porch, and every house is home.
My father taught me how to break the bread
before I spoke, and how to find the river
by sound, and how to keep a stone
in my pocket, and how to wave at a window.

Now I am old and tell it to the window:
the way the whole sky gave up its light,
the way my brother pitched his flat gray stone
too hard and lost it, and we went home
without it, shivering, past the river,
too proud to say we were hungry for bread.

Some hungers do not end with bread.
Some doors have never been a window;
some waters are not only a river.
Whatever I was given of the light
I carried slowly, carefully, back home,
the way you carry something heavy as a stone.

My daughter keeps my brother's lost stone
(she found it, years on) beside the bread
box on the shelf. She says it's what makes home.
I light the lamp and set it in the window
so anyone out walking has the light.
I know the way by heart, down to the river.

Set one stone on the sill of the window,
break the bread and share it in the light,
and every road leads home beside the river.
