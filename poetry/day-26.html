Day 26 - Rhetorical Questions

Rhetorical Questions:
A rhetorical question is a question asked for effect, not to get an answer. The writer already knows the answer, or knows that no answer is possible. Poets use rhetorical questions to make the reader stop and think, to build feeling, and to push an argument. Here are some famous ones:

If Winter comes, can Spring be far behind?
---Percy Bysshe Shelley, "Ode to the West Wind"

If you prick us, do we not bleed? If you tickle us, do we not laugh? If you poison us, do we not die?
---William Shakespeare, The Merchant of Venice

Directions: Write one poem of 8 to 12 lines in which at least five of the lines are rhetorical questions. Choose a topic you care about (a place, a person, an unfair rule, a season). The questions should build on each other. In the last line, you may answer the questions, or leave them hanging so the reader has to answer.
Tips:
1. Make the questions get bigger or more serious as the poem goes on.
2. Repeating the first words of each question ("Who...", "Why...", "How...") gives the poem a beat.
3. Read it aloud. Each question should rise at the end.
Example (written for this backup, not a student example):

Who taught the geese which way is south?
Who tells the maples when to let go?
Who holds the river to its winding path
and calls the hawk back to the same dead pine?

If nobody told them, how do they know?
If everyone knows, why can't I?

Maybe the answer is in the asking.
